What the runs show

Were the ruler to compare the revenue from taxes with the small profits (he reaps from trading himself), he would find the latter negligible in comparison with the former.Ibn Khaldun, The Muqaddimah, chapter 3, section 38, translated by Franz Rosenthal

    Then and now: Tunis, 1377 and 2027

    Six and a half centuries after Ibn Khaldun wrote in Tunis, Tunisia's parliament is examining the 2027 finance bill. It adds no new taxes and leaves the main rates unchanged. Its main measures are reliefs, such as a four-year exemption from income or corporate tax for new firms that declare investments between 2027 and 2030, and lower duties on a first home. It also asks delivery firms to report online sellers' sales to the tax authority, which widens the base rather than raising rates (Business News, November 4, 2026). Tax revenue is still forecast to rise 4.7%, to 49.6 billion dinars (L'Economiste Maghrébin, October 26, 2026). The deficit reaches 14.6 billion dinars, and its financing rests heavily on borrowing at home, including a 9.8 billion dinar interest-free advance from the central bank (Enterprise, November 2, 2026).

    The state is also an owner of firms. The report on public enterprises annexed to the 2024 finance law covered 88 of them, with a combined net loss of 2.98 billion dinars in 2020 and 1.13 billion in 2021. Programmed transfers from the state reached 17.08 billion dinars in 2022 and 14.51 billion in 2023, and 43 enterprises owed the state about 19.7 billion dinars in 2022 while the state owed them more than 7 billion (Business News, April 22, 2026).

    Most of these firms are not a ruler trading for profit. Many run utilities, transport or basic supplies, where a public owner can have good reasons to be present. The model speaks to a narrower question: what happens when a state in financial straits relies on its own commercial activity, or on borrowing, in place of a broad, trusted tax base.

    A historical parallel, not a forecast. The model is not calibrated to Tunisia, and nothing here says whether any Tunisian tax or enterprise is above or below any peak.

    What international practice asks of an owner

    The 2024 Guidelines of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) on the corporate governance of state-owned enterprises read almost as a gloss on Ibn Khaldun's chapter. They ask the state to:

    1. state and regularly review why it owns each enterprise;
    2. keep its role as owner separate from its roles as regulator and policymaker;
    3. let state-owned and private firms compete on equal terms, without hidden advantages in tax, credit or regulation;
    4. cost and pay openly for any public-service obligation, instead of leaving it buried in losses the budget covers later.

    Source: OECD Guidelines on Corporate Governance of State-Owned Enterprises, 2024.

    Method and sources

    This is the model's third version. The first two followed the tax curve alone; this one adds the ruler's trading (from the Muqaddimah, chapter 3, section 38), trust (from its chapter on injustice) and borrowing. The model is stylized: its numbers show how Ibn Khaldun's mechanisms behave together, not the history of any real dynasty or the finances of any modern state.

    The rules of the game
    • The economy can grow up to about twice its starting size. It grows when people keep enough of what they make and shrinks when they keep too little. Public works raise that ceiling a little; without them it is lower.
    • Spending is the court, which grows by the yearly rate set and is reset low by each new dynasty; soldiers, whose cost rises as paid fighters replace the group's own; the public works planned; and interest on any debt.
    • Under the “cover” rule, the rate rises each year by a quarter of whatever gap is not borrowed, up to 90%. Under the “fixed” rule, the rate stays put; soldiers and interest are paid first, then public works, and the court gets what is left.
    • Trust drifts each year toward a level set by two things people can see: the share of spending that comes back to them as public works, and how much of the market the ruler has taken over. When trust falls below 0.7, fewer people pay what they owe; the setting decides how much fewer. What goes unpaid is still produced, outside the tax collector's reach.
    • After the first 15 years, when taxes fall more than 5% short of spending after any borrowing, the ruler takes over part of the market. His trade pays no tax, produces 30% less than the same trade in merchants' hands, and earns the chosen margin. His forced sales and price-fixing weigh on the merchants who remain, as an extra burden worth half his share. Once in, he does not leave; a new dynasty starts without trade.
    • Borrowing, when switched on, covers the chosen share of each year's shortfall until the debt reaches 60% of a year's output. Interest is added to spending every year after. Money lent to the ruler is money not lent to merchants, so each dinar borrowed lowers the economy's capacity slightly. A new dynasty does not take on the old one's debt.
    • Cohesion fades in the capital, faster as the court grows, and grows among outside groups, as in Turchin's model. When an outside group's cohesion exceeds the dynasty's power, it takes over and the economy loses 10% in the fighting.
    • Stages: conquest for the first 15 years; rule by one while cohesion stays above 60% of its start; leisure while taxes stay near their starting level; imitation as they rise; waste once the rate passes the revenue peak or revenue and borrowing together cover less than 90% of spending.

    Assumed values. The size of the court, soldiers and public works, how fast trust moves, how much less productive the ruler's trade is, the burden it puts on merchants, the trigger for trading, and the debt ceiling are all assumptions, set so a dynasty without trading lasts about a century, as Ibn Khaldun said. No one has measured cohesion, trust moves for many reasons the model leaves out, and how strongly people respond to taxes is debated even for modern economies.
