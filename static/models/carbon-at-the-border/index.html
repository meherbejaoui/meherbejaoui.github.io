What the numbers show

With verified data, a typical plant, the third-quarter 2026 certificate price of €82, no Tunisian carbon price and half the cost passed on to EU buyers, unless stated.

  • €19 → €72

    The clinker bill almost quadruples with no change at the kiln

    A typical kiln at 0.88 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne owes about €19 a tonne in 2026, already close to two-fifths of a €50 export price. As the free share falls, it owes €44 in 2030 and €72 in 2034. The Commission's July 2026 proposal, if adopted, would soften 2034 to about €64.

  • €1.8m → €5.4m

    Proof is worth more each year for cement

    Tunisia's official default for grey clinker equals the typical kiln, so a cement exporter without data pays only the mark-up: €26 instead of €19 a tonne in 2026, and €45 instead of €23 from 2028. On 250,000 tonnes a year, verified data saves about €1.8 million in 2026 and €5.4 million in 2028.

  • €9 vs €251

    Steel without data is judged as if it came from a blast furnace

    Tunisia has no default values for steel, so a scrap-based mill without data is charged on the "other countries" figure of almost 4 tonnes of CO₂. It owes €251 a tonne in 2026 instead of €9, close to half the export price.

  • €44 vs €92

    A home carbon price only counts with verified data

    In 2030, a Tunisian price of €30 a tonne of CO₂ collects about €26 per tonne of clinker at home and cuts the EU payment from €44 to €18, if the plant's emissions are verified. Without verified data, nothing can be deducted, because no default carbon price exists for Tunisia: the same tonne pays €26 at home and €66 to the EU.

  • 3% → 7%

    For fertilizer the bill is real but small next to the price

    At Tunisia's official default of 0.52 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of diammonium phosphate, the bill rises from €16 in 2026 to €43 in 2034, between 3% and 7% of a €600 price. Made with low-carbon ammonia, the product owes nothing until 2031.

  • €36 per MWh

    Gas-fired electricity gets no free share at all

    At Tunisia's default of 0.436 tonnes of CO₂ per megawatt-hour, power sent to Italy through the ELMED cable would owe about €36 per megawatt-hour from the first day, more than a third of a €100 wholesale price. Solar power sold under a direct contract, and proven as such, would owe nothing.

  • 3 levers

    The price is set in Brussels; the evidence is made in Tunisia

    Three things decide the bill that Tunisia can change: how clean its plants are, whether they can prove it, and whether carbon is priced at home. The second unlocks the other two. A clean plant without verified data pays as if it were average or worse, and a Tunisian carbon price paid on unverified emissions is paid twice.

The state at the border

In each of the four products here, the state owns at least one of the producers that matter. Tunisia supplied 7.9% of the cement and clinker the EU imported in the first half of 2026, about 400,000 tonnes (CemNet, citing Cement Europe).

  • CementLes Ciments de BizerteMajority state-owned. It produced no clinker in 2025; on March 17, 2026 the government decided to restructure it and raise its clinker and cement exports.
  • SteelEl Fouladh, Menzel BourguibaThe only integrated steelmaker, melting scrap in electric furnaces. It is being restructured without opening its capital, with a 330 million dinar plan to triple output to about 300,000 tonnes.
  • FertilizerGroupe Chimique TunisienThe state's phosphate processor, which makes diammonium phosphate among other products, with state-guaranteed financing for its inputs.
  • ElectricitySTEG and ELMEDThe state utility is building the 600-megawatt cable to Sicily with Italy's Terna. It is expected in service between 2028 and 2030.

Where the producer is public, CBAM is a question for the owner as much as for the firm. The cost of missing data, of an old kiln or of an unrecognized carbon price lands on a public balance sheet, and the decisions that change it (measurement, verification, investment, a home carbon price) are decisions the owner can require. Tunisia has studied a carbon tax since 2025 but has not adopted one; the 2027 finance bill cuts duties on solar panels to 10% and exempts storage batteries instead.

The model is not calibrated to any of these firms. Their actual emissions, export volumes and prices are not public in a form the model could use.

Method and sources

A policy calculator, not a simulation: the page applies the EU's published formula to one unit of an export, year by year. This is its second version. It now applies the rule that a carbon price paid in Tunisia can be deducted only with verified emissions, and its data were checked on .

The rules of the game
  • Certificates owed = emissions counted − (share still free × EU yardstick). The share still free falls from 97.5% in 2026 to 51.5% in 2030 and 0% in 2034. The correction factor applied to EU free allocation is taken as 100%.
  • With verified data, the emissions counted are the plant's own, including the electricity it uses for cement and fertilizers. Without, they are the official default value for the country of origin plus the mark-up: 10% in 2026, 20% in 2027 and 30% from 2028 for cement and steel; 1% for fertilizers; none for electricity.
  • Tunisia has its own default values for cement, fertilizers, aluminium and hydrogen, but not for steel, so Tunisian steel without data takes the value for "other countries and territories."
  • For steel, the yardstick depends on how the steel is made: 0.066 tonnes for scrap melted in an electric furnace, 1.364 for the blast-furnace route that comes with the default value.
  • A carbon price paid in Tunisia is deducted up to the amount owed, and only when the emissions are verified. With default values, only a default carbon price published by the Commission could be deducted, and none exists for Tunisia. Anything paid beyond the amount owed is not refunded.
  • The EU price is held at the level set for every year. For goods imported in 2026, the certificate price is the average EU auction price of the quarter of import; from 2027 it is set weekly. Importers bringing in less than 50 tonnes of these goods a year are exempt, except for electricity and hydrogen.
  • Who bears the cost is a single share set by hand: what EU buyers accept in a higher price, the rest coming off the exporter's price.

How sure is this? The rules, yardsticks, mark-ups, phase-out, Tunisia's default values and certificate prices are taken from EU regulations and the Commission's published prices. The Tunisian rows were read from a machine-readable copy of Annex I that matches the official text for every row checked; confirm them in the Commission's spreadsheet before using a figure in a contract. The deduction rules were reported adopted on November 5, 2026, but the final text was not yet readable; the page follows the published draft. The emissions of individual Tunisian plants and the export prices are assumptions.
