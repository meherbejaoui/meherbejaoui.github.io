Around the Tunisian revolution of 2011, President Zine El Abidine Ben Ali said, in substance, "I'm not a sun to shine over everything". He was blaming the people around him, who, he said, had misled him and hidden the country's social and economic reality until it exploded. It was a self-serving line from a man whose regime had built that very circle. Over the years, though, I came to think he had described something real, even if he had no standing to complain about it. The files did reach his desk. They had simply spent years on other desks first.

This article is about those other desks.

## The gate

In public institutions, reforms often stall and can take years to cross the finish line. Supervisory bodies, the agencies that control or review the work of others, cannot do that work well if their own internal structures and ways of working are not properly governed. Reforming them is harder still. They sit higher up, and they have the access and the capacity to set the agenda, influence people, and lobby behind the scenes. A project to reform such an institution can sit in someone's drawer for as long as that someone wants, and in practice that someone is a network of people. Eventually, the reform becomes one more file subject to the administrative cycle of life, and to the harsh reality of being waited out.

Higher up an institution, fewer people hold more knowledge and more power. If they are career officials, their power is even more pronounced than that of political appointees and elected officials. Yet any half-wise government official, especially in a developing country, will tell you that it is never a good idea to argue with the higher-ups or tell them no, particularly if they were politically chosen. That is where the institution's inherited wisdom comes in: there are a thousand and one ways to say no without ever saying it.

Political scientists Peter Bachrach and Morton Baratz gave this a name. In two articles from the early 1960s, they argued that winning decisions is only the visible face of power. Its second face works quietly, by settling what never gets decided. They called it nondecision-making: keeping decisions confined to "safe" issues by shaping the values, myths, procedures and institutions that determine what reaches the table at all. On this view, a non-decision is still a decision. It simply leaves no record.

Sometimes the filtering is justified. An issue may not be a priority, may not fit the context, or may run against the concerns of more important stakeholders. But it is easy to fall into the false narrative behind this. A passing minister or elected official does not necessarily know much of what is needed or happening inside a high-level agency, and I would argue they are not supposed to know all its intricacies. That gap is exactly where the power held by the few shapes the safe arena where most decisions, and most eyes, will go.

Think of that arena as a gated community, with restricted access and guards who decide what and who gets through. The filtering includes the mail. If bills, deliveries and guests cannot pass, the residents never learn what is happening outside, or what is really being asked of them.

## The drawer

The gate is the part Bachrach and Baratz described best. In their 1970 book, they also noted what can happen to a demand that gets past it: it can be "maimed or destroyed in the decision-implementing stage". But they treated this as a fallback, what the powerful do once the gate has failed. In public administration, I would argue it is the main event.

One way it works is by weakening the drafts that come in, so that a reform arrives thinner in substance and disfigured from what it was meant to do. In an earlier paper on Tunisia's budget reform, I called one version of this "Tunisification": a reform borrowed from abroad and then tweaked at every level of the political and administrative machinery until it loses its core and its reason for existing. Another way is to shape the communities involved in the issue, and the narratives that surround it.

This may sound bigger than it is. In fact, it is more common and more natural than it first appears. Reforms are change, and we humans do not really like change. A file that gets past the gate can stall for years, even when everyone who passes it glances at it or reads it in full. Whether it is a difficult issue or a minor fix, it can still be made to look tricky, risky, untimely, unpopular, or anything else that justifies letting it sit.

This is a distinct kind of non-decision, and only highly qualified people, or those with deep institutional knowledge, can make it and survive. It requires precise knowledge of the institution itself, of the applicable laws and regulations, of the procedures, and of the dynamics around the file and the place. It is always curious to watch. The effort is real, and the skill is real, but both are spent on not deciding, and the potential is wasted.

## Why it lasts

Other forces in the room pull in the same direction. In public institutions, the drawer also becomes a force that pushes capable people out the door. The organization's memory is shaped by a vicious cycle: capable insiders filter the agenda and defer decisions, work hard to avoid blame, and then push out the other capable people who might object or become a thorn in their side. What remains is a safe space where all is well.

The passing official does not see this picture, and in many cases has no incentive to fix it. They have a mandate to deliver, and once the others have left, the only capable people available are those who will not decide, and who are glad to send files upward for signature or across to other institutions. I built a [simulation of this decline spiral](https://www.meherbejaoui.com/institutions/simulation/), and the [reading map](https://www.meherbejaoui.com/institutions/reading-map/) places it alongside other explanations, including Hirschman's exit and voice and Hood's work on blame avoidance.

Which brings me back to Ben Ali. In developing countries, and perhaps in institutions elsewhere, signing off on a tough decision is a risk: of losing something, or of making a mistake the organization or its partners will punish. The president of the time did receive important dossiers. But before reaching him, they had sat on other desks and gone through rigorous checks and approvals, meticulous studies and benchmarking, and thorough legal and economic assessments. That is enough time for a ministerial rotation or two. Those files made it through the gate, and then met an even stronger force on the other side.

These may look like simple cases of malicious compliance on steroids. More often, they hide a reality of administrative malfunction and decline. And they carry a lesson for anyone trying to reform an institution: opening the agenda is not enough. A reform that only widens the gate will reach the desk and then wait in the drawer, unless the institution still has people with the capacity, and the security, to sign.
