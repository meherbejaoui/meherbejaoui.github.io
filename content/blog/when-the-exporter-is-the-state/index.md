Since January 1, 2026, a tonne of cement clinker shipped from Tunisia to the European Union (EU) carries a carbon bill. The importer declares the carbon dioxide released to make it, subtracts the share that European producers still receive free, and buys certificates for the rest at the EU's carbon price. Certificate sales open on February 1, 2027, and the first annual declaration for 2026 imports is due on September 30, 2027. The mechanism is called the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), and it covers cement, fertilizers, iron and steel, aluminium, hydrogen and electricity.

Tunisian commentary has mostly treated CBAM as a problem for exporters. That is accurate as far as it goes. It leaves out a fact that changes who has to act: in each of the product lines Tunisia sells or plans to sell to Europe under CBAM, the state owns at least one of the producers that matter.

## A tax that works like an audit

On paper, CBAM is a price. In practice, it is a test of evidence, and the price follows from how the test goes.

The bill depends on three numbers. The first is the plant's emissions per tonne. If the exporter can supply them, measured under EU methods and checked by an accredited verifier, the importer declares them. If not, the importer must use a default value that the European Commission publishes for each product and country of origin, plus a mark-up of 10% in 2026, 20% in 2027 and 30% from 2028 for cement and steel. The second is a yardstick: EU producers still receive free allowances up to a benchmark set on the cleanest European plants, and imports get the same deduction, on a share that falls from 97.5% in 2026 to zero in 2034. The third is any carbon price already paid in the country of origin, which can be deducted.

Each of the three rewards proof. Tunisia's default for grey clinker is 0.88 tonnes of carbon dioxide (CO₂) per tonne, close to a typical kiln, so a cement exporter without data loses only the mark-up. Steel is different. Tunisia has no default values for steel at all, so Tunisian steel without data is charged on the figure for "other countries and territories," set for the blast-furnace route at almost 4 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne. A mill that melts scrap in an electric furnace, at a fraction of that, would be charged as if it ran a blast furnace.

The rules on deducting a carbon price paid abroad make the same point more sharply. The Commission's draft, published on May 13, 2026, allows the deduction on default emission values only through a default carbon price that the Commission itself publishes for the country. The act was reportedly adopted on November 5, 2026, after member states failed to give it a majority in committee in September. Tunisia has no carbon price, so no default carbon price exists for it. A Tunisian carbon tax paid on unverified emissions would therefore be paid twice: once at home, and again at the border.

## The state on the other side of the border

The European debate on CBAM pictures a private firm weighing a new cost. In Tunisia, the firm is often the state.

Cement is the clearest case. Tunisia supplied 7.9% of the cement and clinker the EU imported in the first half of 2026, out of almost 5 million tonnes. Les Ciments de Bizerte, majority owned by the state since its partial listing in 2009, produced no clinker in 2025, and in its third quarter of 2026 it was buying clinker to grind into cement. On March 17, 2026, a restricted ministerial council decided to restructure it, with the long-term aim of developing its port and raising its exports of clinker and cement. The same council decided to restructure El Fouladh, the state steelmaker in Menzel Bourguiba and Tunisia's only integrated producer, which melts scrap in electric arc furnaces. The government has ruled out opening its capital. A 330 million dinar project under discussion with the Islamic Development Bank would triple its output to about 300,000 tonnes a year, and bids for a new electric steelworks were due on November 17, 2026. Fertilizer runs through the Groupe Chimique Tunisien (GCT), the state's phosphate processor, which makes diammonium phosphate among other products and whose input financing the state guarantees. Electricity, the newest candidate, runs through the Société Tunisienne de l'Electricité et du Gaz (STEG), which is building the 600-megawatt ELMED cable to Sicily with Italy's Terna, now expected in service between 2028 and 2030.

The official account of these decisions, as reported, is about capacity, ports, rail, scrap supply and sovereignty. In practice, the value of every tonne these firms export to Europe from now on also depends on whether its emissions can be measured, verified and declared. None of the public reports on the two restructurings that I could find mentions emissions measurement or verification. That is not proof that the plans ignore it. It does mean the question is not part of how the decisions are explained.

At the third-quarter 2026 certificate price of €82.32, a typical kiln owes about €19 per tonne of clinker in 2026, already close to two-fifths of a €50 export price. Nothing has to change at the plant for the bill to grow. As the free share falls, the same tonne owes €44 in 2030 and €72 in 2034. A proposal the Commission made in July 2026 to slow the phase-out would soften 2034 to about €64, if Parliament and Council agree to it.

The value of evidence grows on the same curve. On 250,000 tonnes of clinker a year, verified data saves about €1.8 million in 2026 and €5.4 million from 2028, when the mark-up reaches 30%. For steel the gap is of another order: a tonne of scrap-based rebar owes about €9 with verified data and €251 without, close to half its export price. El Fouladh sells at home today, and its plan is to replace imports, so CBAM does not reach it now. The point concerns what the state is building. An electric furnace on scrap is among the cleanest ways to make steel, and also the route on which, without data, Tunisian steel would be charged as the dirtiest.

The carbon price result is the one that bears most directly on public finance. In 2030, a Tunisian price of €30 per tonne of CO₂ would collect about €26 per tonne of clinker at home and cut the EU payment from €44 to €18, if the kiln's emissions are verified. The exporter pays the same €44 either way, but €26 of it stays in Tunisia. Without verified data, the same tonne pays €26 at home and €66 at the border. A carbon tax designed to keep revenue in the country would, without measurement, mostly raise costs.

## What only an owner can decide

Three things decide the bill that Tunisia can change: how clean its plants are, whether they can prove it, and whether carbon is priced at home. For a private exporter, the first two are business decisions. For Les Ciments de Bizerte, El Fouladh or the GCT, they are decisions the owner can require, fund and monitor, and the cost of not taking them lands on a public balance sheet, through lower export earnings or higher transfers from the budget.

International practice already expects the owner to act. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) added a chapter on sustainability to its Guidelines on Corporate Governance of State-Owned Enterprises in 2024. Where the state has sustainability goals, it asks the state as owner to set concrete expectations for its enterprises, including on disclosure, and to encourage credible climate transition plans. The OECD's own survey found that only 26% of jurisdictions explicitly include climate goals in their ownership policy, so an owner that did so would be early, not late.

Paradoxically, the measure that matters most is the least visible. Measurement and verification cost little next to a new kiln or furnace, and they come before both: a plant cannot show that it is cleaner than the default without them, and a home carbon price cannot be deducted without them. An owner restructuring two plants at once, with one new furnace out to tender, has the rare chance to write monitoring into the project from the start rather than add it to an old installation later. The price is set in Brussels. The evidence is made in Tunisia, and in these firms, by the state.

Tunisia has studied a carbon tax since 2025 but has not adopted one, and its 2027 finance bill takes a different route, cutting customs duties on solar panels to 10% and exempting storage batteries. That is a reasonable start for a country whose electricity is mostly gas-fired. It does not answer the border question, which concerns the factories.

## What is still moving

Several of the rules are not settled. Updated EU yardsticks, based on the final 2026–2030 benchmarks of the EU emissions trading system (ETS), are expected for goods imported from January 1, 2027. They are not yet adopted, and the final clinker benchmark (0.656) is slightly lower than the one in force (0.666), which would raise the clinker bill by under €1 a tonne in 2030. Parliament and Council are due to hold their second negotiating round on November 30, 2026 on extending CBAM, from 2028, to between about 180 and 457 steel and aluminium products, such as screws, wire and tubes. The ETS review, which would slow the phase-out of free allowances to 2038, enters negotiations in January 2027. None of these changes the direction of the argument. They change the size of the numbers, and the calculator will follow them.

## Notes on the model

The calculator is a policy calculator, not a simulation: it applies the published formula to one unit of an export. It is not calibrated to any Tunisian firm, because their actual emissions, export volumes and prices are not public in a form it could use. The typical clinker and fertilizer plants are set to Tunisia's official default totals, the steel mill is an assumed scrap-based electric furnace, and the share of the cost passed on to European buyers is a single assumed figure. The EU carbon price is held constant across years at the level chosen. The deduction rules follow the Commission's published draft, since the adopted text was not yet readable when the page was checked on November 11, 2026. Every official value, with its source, is listed on the model's page under "Method and sources."
